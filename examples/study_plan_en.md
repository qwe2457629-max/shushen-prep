# Study Plan

## 1. Why I Want to Study Sustainability Management

In my third year, our environmental economics instructor asked us to find out how much electricity our own campus used. My team obtained 2022–2024 electricity data for 12 campus buildings. We expected a simple summary table. Instead, we found that two buildings used only 15% less power on weekends than on weekdays, even though almost no classes were held. When we went to look, we learned that several old air-conditioning units had kept their original schedules since installation.

That project showed me that saving energy is often not about new technology. It is about whether someone reads the data and asks why the numbers look the way they do. I want to practice this, finding a problem in the data and then getting people to fix it, in settings larger than a campus.

## 2. What I Have Done So Far

- **Capstone project on campus electricity use** (Feb–Dec 2025, team of three). I cleaned three years of billing data and wrote Python scripts to plot each building's load profile. We recommended new air-conditioning schedules for two buildings. After a one-semester trial, weekend use in those buildings fell by about 9%.
- **Summer internship** (Jul–Aug 2025, engineering department of a property management company). I compiled meter data for five office buildings into monthly reports. One building showed identical readings for three months in a row. I reported it, the engineers found a faulty meter, and the earlier reports had to be recalculated.
- **Coursework**: statistics, econometrics, environmental economics and operations research. Econometrics taught me that lower consumption does not prove a measure worked; a cooler summer can do the same. We later added temperature to our capstone analysis for that reason.
- **English**: GEPT High-Intermediate. I read English research reports comfortably, and I am still working on my academic writing.

## 3. Research Interest

During my internship I noticed that the company hired consultants every year to run energy audits. The reports were thorough, but few recommendations were carried out. The engineering supervisor put it simply: "The owner pays for new equipment, but the tenants pay the electricity bill. The owner saves nothing, so why would he upgrade?"

My question is: **why do energy-saving recommendations for office buildings so often stay on paper?** Specifically:

1. Under what conditions are owners, tenants and property managers willing to pay or act?
2. Does the way a lease splits electricity costs affect whether measures are implemented?
3. In buildings where upgrades did happen, how was the deal worked out?

I plan to interview property managers and owner representatives from 8–10 buildings and compare their answers with public building energy statistics. The question is still rough, and I hope to sharpen both the question and the method with my advisor's guidance.

## 4. Study Timeline

- **Before enrollment (Feb–Aug 2027)**: strengthen statistics and Python; read key studies on building energy efficiency and lease incentives, and keep structured notes.
- **First year**: complete the required courses and take electives in energy policy, environmental economics and qualitative methods. Find an advisor in the first semester; finalize the interview list and questions in the second, with 2–3 pilot interviews.
- **Second year**: finish interviews and analysis in the first semester and write the thesis in the second, aiming to graduate on time.

## 5. After Graduation

I hope to work in energy management at a property management or energy services company, where my job would be to turn audit recommendations into projects that actually happen. In graduate school I want to learn not only how to calculate the savings, but also how to convince the people who pay.
